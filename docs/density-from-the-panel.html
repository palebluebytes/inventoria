Inventoria · Density Class ADR-0108 §2 · ticket #505

The Panel Knows the Weight

A bottle publishes its figures per 100 ml. To show you grams, the app needs grams per millilitre — and nothing on the label states one. It does not guess from the product’s name, and it does not ask you. It reads three numbers off the nutrition panel. Here is why that works, and how you can check it rather than take it.

A nutrition panel is a density measurement written in a different unit. It says how many grams of fat, sugar and protein sit in 100 g of food; the remainder is water. Each of those has a known density. Add up the volumes and the weight of a millilitre falls out — no name, no language, no model.

The idea

Density is a mixing problem, and the panel is the recipe

Nothing in a bottle is exotic. Four things make up almost all of any drinkable food, and each has a density that has been known for a century:

Component densities, g/ml at room temperature the whole of the physics
Component g/ml What it does to the weight
Fat 0.920 Lightens — floats on water
Water 0.998 The baseline
Protein 1.350 Heavies, but is never much of a drink
Sugar (sucrose) 1.590 Heavies hard — dissolves and packs in
A panel gives grams of fat, carbohydrate and protein per 100 g of food. What is left over is water. Divide each component’s grams by its density to get its volume, add the volumes, and 100 g ÷ that volume is the food’s density. That is it. No fitting, no training, no corpus.

Proof I Separation

The three kinds do not overlap — not even close

The reference tables state a volume portion and its weight in grams for 124 of the foods the app ships. Dividing one by the other gives a measured density for each: not a model’s opinion, a laboratory figure. Plotted on one axis, they fall into three clumps with visible air between them.

124 measured densities, g/ml one tick per food · acid mark = the figure the app pins
The widest oil is 0.9468. The lightest drink is 0.9806. The heaviest drink is 1.0482 and the lightest syrup is 1.2976. There is no food in either gap. A class here is not a bucket somebody drew — it is a cluster that was already there.
The rule

Nine lines, and the whole of what the app decides

Because the clumps are separated, telling them apart needs no cleverness. Three comparisons put a food in one of them, and the class carries the pinned figure. This is the actual shipped code, from src/lib/food/density.ts: 

// Gated by the caller on a volume basis: a per-100-g panel never reaches here.
export function densityClassFromPanel(panel): DensityClassId | undefined {
  const kcal = panel.calories;
  if (typeof kcal !== "number") return undefined;
  const fat  = panel.fat_content ?? 0;
  const carb = panel.carbohydrate_content ?? 0;
  if (fat >= 80) return "oil";                       // 0.92 g/ml
  if (kcal >= 250 && fat < 5 && carb >= 55) return "syrup"; // 1.39 g/ml
  return "liquid";                                  // 1.00 g/ml
}

Note what is not in there. No description. No product_name. The whole file contains zero references to either, which is why a Bulgarian olive oil and a Portuguese one classify identically: 91.6 g of fat is 91.6 g of fat in every language.

Proof II Margin

Each threshold sits in an empty corridor, not on a boundary

This is the part worth dwelling on, because it is what makes the rule robust rather than merely correct today. A fitted classifier puts its boundary where the classes meet. These three lines are nowhere near anything. Each sits in a wide band of values that no food in the corpus occupies at all.

Where each line sits, against where the foods are solid = occupied · open = nothing here · acid = the shipped threshold
The fat line could be drawn anywhere from 8 to 98 and not one food would change class. That is a margin of 92 percentage points on a scale of 100.

Which is testable directly: move the thresholds, or delete them, and count how many of the 124 measured foods end up assigned a figure further from their own measurement than a different class’s figure would have been.

Six variants of the rule, scored on the same 124 foods wrong = a nearer figure existed and was not chosen

Every variant scores zero, and that is the finding — the thresholds are not load-bearing, the separation is. The extra clauses are not tuning; they are there so a food the corpus does not contain (a cream liqueur, a nut butter thinned into a sauce) cannot walk into the syrup branch on one number alone.
Proof III Physics

The composition predicts the measurement to two decimal places

Separation says the classes are real. This says the panel is genuinely reading the density rather than correlating with it. Take the mixing arithmetic from the top of this page, run it on all 124 foods using only their panels, and compare against the laboratory figure.

Predicted from the panel, against measured in a lab 124 foods · the diagonal is a perfect prediction
Root-mean-square error 0.0178 g/ml across the lot, from nothing but the panel. And the predicted medians land on the pinned figures almost exactly: 0.9200 against a pinned 0.92, 1.0082 against 1.00, 1.3893 against 1.39. The app did not choose those three numbers to fit the data — mixing physics chose them, and the corpus agreed.

The cost of being wrong

What it costs when the figure is off, in the only unit that matters

A class figure is an average, so it is wrong about every individual food by a little. The question is never “is it exact” — it is “how many kilocalories does the error buy or lose on something a person would actually pour?” Priced at each food’s own energy density:

3.5kcal on a tbsp
worst oil — corn and canola, 0.9468
3.8kcal on a tbsp
worst syrup — high-fructose corn, 1.2976
4.6kcal on a 100 ml glass
worst drink — late-harvest white, 1.0415
10kcal — the bar below which
the app may not ask a question

Every one clears the bar, and the bar itself is the interesting decision: ten kilocalories is beneath the width of the answers this app already gives you. Your panel is rounded to whole kilocalories. Your pour was an estimate. A question that buys less than that buys nothing you can perceive — so it is not asked.

One caveat stated plainly rather than buried: on a full 250 ml of that late-harvest dessert wine the error reaches 11.6 kcal, over the bar. Nobody drinks a 250 ml glass of dessert wine, which is why the figure above is priced on 100 ml — but the larger number exists and this is where it is written down.

There is also one food where the rule and the name disagree, and it is the best single argument in favour of reading numbers:

Sugar-free syrup is 51 kcal per 100 g and really does weigh 1.0144 — the panel is right and the word on the jar is a marketing term. A model reading the label would have to know that; the rule cannot help knowing it. The name-matching regexes in this repo exist only to select which reference foods a pinned figure was averaged over, at build time. They never run against a product you scan.
And here is the real hole

Two things a panel physically cannot report, because neither is a nutrient. Air: an ice cream’s panel is a custard’s, so nothing in it says the tub is half whipped air near 0.65 g/ml. Dilution: a squash’s panel is a juice’s — both are sugar-water — and nothing in it says you are going to add four parts water. A cordial nearer 1.20 read as a drink at 1.00 is about 90 kcal out on a 300 ml pour, nine times the bar.

For those the app refuses to answer and asks you instead, gated on the source’s own category tags. Which is where it is genuinely weak: 48,922 of the 201,821 millilitre products in Open Food Facts — 24.2% — carry no usable category tags at all, and they are ordinary things: Orangina, Red Bull, Bière 33cl, Martini Rosato. An untagged cordial in that share gets read from its panel and weighed wrong. Nothing in a panel can close that gap, and this is the one place in the whole feature where reading the words on the label — in whatever language they are printed — would earn its keep.

The obvious next question

Then why keep the table? Why not just compute it?

If the mixing arithmetic predicts a density to 0.0178 g/ml from the panel, the three pinned figures look redundant. Compute a density for every bottle from its own label and throw the table away. It is the right question to ask, and it was worth measuring rather than arguing — so both were scored against the same 124 laboratory figures.

The answer flips depending on which unit you score in.

The class table against per-food mixing physics same 124 foods · kcal priced at each food’s own energy
Approach RMS g/ml Mean error Worst case Closest on
Class table — three pinned figures 0.0211 1.44 kcal 11.6 kcal 96 of 124
Mixing physics — computed per food 0.0178 1.53 kcal 21.9 kcal 28 of 124
The physics wins on g/ml and loses on kilocalories, which is the unit every decision in this app is priced in. It is also closer on only 23% of foods. The reason is that a pinned figure is the median of real measurements, so it quietly absorbs everything the model gets wrong; the physics is an honest description of an idealised liquid, and the median is an empirical fit to actual ones.

One food carries most of that difference, and it is the whole argument in a single bottle:

Alcoholic beverage, wine, dessert, sweet the case that decides it
Measured in a lab 0.9975 What the bottle actually weighs.
Class table says 1.00 Out by 1.0 kcal on a glass.
Mixing physics says 1.0522 Out by 21.9 kcal — twice the bar.
Ethanol is 0.789 g/ml, and it is not a macronutrient. It appears nowhere on a nutrition panel. The model computes water as 100 − fat − carb − protein, so it counts the alcohol’s mass as water, reads a dessert wine as sugar-water and predicts it heavy. Every wine, beer and spirit breaks the same way — and those are precisely the things people pour 250 ml of. The table never had to know alcohol exists, because its figures were measured on real bottles with the alcohol already in them.

Then there is the data the app is actually fed. Open Food Facts panels are entered by people, and fields go missing. Deleting one field from all 124 foods:

What a missing field does to each approach mean error over the 124, after the field is removed
Field removed Foods that change class Table Physics
Protein 0 1.44 kcal 1.64 kcal
Carbohydrate 9 21.6 kcal 21.5 kcal
Fat 41 59.5 kcal 58.1 kcal
The rule reads fat, carbohydrate and energy, so a missing protein field is invisible to it — zero foods change class, while the physics degrades, because protein is a term in its sum. Stated honestly, the two shaded rows are a draw: strip carbohydrate or fat and both approaches collapse. What differs is the shape of the failure. A wrong class is discrete, visible and one tap from being corrected; a wrong computed figure is a plausible number nobody can spot.

And it would take the correction away. Three cells work because they name things you can recognise while holding the bottle — a drink, an oil, a syrup. If the app had computed 1.0522 for that wine, disagreeing with it would mean typing the right grams per millilitre, which is exactly the trade ADR-0108 §12 refuses: being asked to validate a figure you have no way to check.

There is nothing to gate, either. The three figures are re-derived from the shipped corpus on every build, and the build stops if one moves. A formula has no corpus to check against — it would be four constants and an ideal-mixing assumption, trusted on faith.

A middle option was tested too, and also refused: use the gap between the physics and the class figure as a signal to ask. It correlates with the table’s real error at r = 0.62, and flagging the worst 10% catches 31% of the total error against 10% for asking at random. Three times better than chance — but four of the six foods it flags hardest are ones the table gets right to within 1.8 kcal, and the total error it is chasing averages 1.44 kcal a food. Thirteen questions to recover a fraction of something already under the bar is the exact trade #505 removed.

So what is the physics for

It is the explanation, not the implementation. It is why 0.92, 1.00 and 1.39 are the right three numbers rather than three arbitrary ones, and it is what showed that the classes are real corners of a composition space rather than buckets somebody drew. The syrup residual is the clearest case: 0.0453 g/ml, the worst of the three, because concentrated sugar solutions genuinely contract when mixed. Ideal mixing over-predicts them, and the measured median absorbs what the model cannot express.

One gap remains open and it is the same one the tag census exposes: all 124 of these foods are USDA reference records. Neither approach has yet been scored against real Open Food Facts panels at scale, which is the measurement worth taking before anything here is revisited.

Why “provably” and not “probably”

The figures are re-measured on every build, or the build fails

Three numbers — 0.92, 1.00, 1.39 — are written down in density-class.ts, together with the pattern that selected the foods each was averaged over and the statistics that admitted it: how many foods, how wide the spread, the coefficient of variation. Nothing computes them at runtime, on purpose, because a figure computed from a corpus that gets regenerated would drift between releases with nobody deciding it should.

Instead scripts/density-class-check.mjs re-selects those foods out of the shipped corpus on every pnpm check and re-derives every figure. If the corpus changes and a median moves, the build stops. The 124 measurements on this page are the same ones that gate runs on, and you can run it yourself: 

$ pnpm check:density
  ok  3 Density Class figures still measure what they were pinned at (238 portions),
      and the 1 refused classes still fail the bar

That last clause matters too. A fourth class — spirits — was measured, failed on spread, and was refused; the gate keeps checking that it still fails, because a refusal that quietly became wrong is as much a silence as a figure that quietly moved.

Figures on this page were measured against the shipped search index, schema 10, 2,023 reference foods — 124 of them stating both a volume portion and its weight. Component densities are standard room-temperature values; the mixing model assumes ideal mixing, which is why syrups carry the largest residual (0.0453 g/ml RMS): concentrated sugar solutions genuinely do not mix ideally. Sources: USDA FoodData Central (Foundation and SR Legacy) for every measurement, Open Food Facts for the 4,747,804-product tag census. Decisions and their reasoning live in ADR-0108 and its 2026-09-30 amendment; the code is src/lib/food/density.ts and src/lib/food/density-class.ts.
