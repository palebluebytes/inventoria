01 · The short answer

Three narrowings, two of them invisible

Search is not one filter. It is a membership decision taken at build time, a retrieval decision taken on your keystroke, and an ordering decision taken on what survived both. Most of the work — 47% of everything USDA published — happened before you opened the app.

The whole pipeline is local. There is no search server, no API key and no network call: a committed JSON file ships with the app, is parsed in about 14 ms, and every keystroke ranks all 4,238 rows in memory. Search works on a plane.

02 · Sources

Four places a food can come from

Only one of them is a text search. Knowing which is which explains most of what looks inconsistent about the screen.

SourceReached bySizeWhat it is for
USDA reference foodstyping a word4,238Base ingredients — the things you cook with. The only text-searchable table.
Open Food Factsscanning a barcode—Packaged products. Deliberately not text-searchable. No packet, no product.
Curated stand-instyping a word2Hand-captured products for base foods USDA lacks: cacao nibs, double cream. Capped at 8.
Your own ledgerRecent list—Anything you logged before. Sorted by time, not ranked.

When you type, the curated table and the USDA corpus are searched with the same phrases, and the results are stitched in a fixed order: exact curated hits first, then every USDA row, then partial curated hits. So typing cacao nibs leads with the stand-in, while a broad cocoa never displaces USDA's cocoa powder.

Why products are not text-searchable

Open Food Facts is crowd-sourced and enormous, and a text search over it would bury a plain onion under forty branded onion products. Barcode-only is a deliberate refusal, not a missing feature — and it is a known cost: if the packet is already in the bin, the product is unreachable.

03 · The spine

Membership is decided once; retrieval is decided every keystroke

This split is the single most useful thing to hold onto. Two completely different sets of rules run at two completely different times, and confusing them is how almost every question about search goes wrong.

MembershipRetrieval & order
WhenOnce, when the artifact is generatedEvery keystroke, in your browser
QuestionIs this a food a person logs?Does this word reach it, and how well?
Instrument14 drop rules + 4 name rosters6 tier rungs + 10 ordering keys
FailureA food is gone and nothing says soA food is there but buried
Recoverable?No — you cannot scroll to a row that never shippedYes — by scrolling

That asymmetry is why the rules are lopsided, and why the rest of this page is mostly about the left-hand column. A wrong order costs a scroll. A wrong drop costs the food, silently, with nothing on screen to notice.

04 · The review surface

What was discarded

3,736 of USDA's 7,974 records never reach the app — 47% of everything published. A dropped record is simply absent from the shipped index, with nothing anywhere saying which rule took it. This section is the reconstruction: every one of them, the rule that removed it, and the terms that rule fired on.

The cause is found by ablation, not by reading a table. The filters' word lists are deliberately private, so a page that copied them would be a second copy of two hundred lines of editorial judgement, drifting quietly. Instead each record is asked of the real rule with terms removed one at a time, and what is reported is the minimal sufficient removal set: the smallest set of terms you could delete and have the rule go quiet. It survives a rewrite of the rules.

08 · Keystroke, part one

What your word reaches

USDA names a food Food, qualifier, qualifier. Everything below leans on that: the head phrase before the first comma is the food's identity, and everything after it is description. Retrieval scores how much of a food's own name your query accounts for, on six rungs.

The tier ladder — strongest first, as the list reads

50The head phrase is your query.“grape” → Grapes, muscadine, raw
40A typed word is a head word.“grape” → Grape leaves, raw
30The head completes what you are still typing.“pot” → Potatoes, flesh and skin, raw
20A whole typed word, but only in a qualifier.“grape” → Tomatoes, grape, raw
10A typed word merely prefixes some word, anywhere.“pot” → Sweet potato leaves, raw
0No match at all. The row never enters the result set.the row is not in the result set

Rung 40 sits above rung 30 deliberately: for “grape”, grape leaves are a grape and a grapefruit is not.

The stray-mention rule

USDA writes an ingredient where a qualifier goes, so Cheese, mozzarella, whole milk matches the word milk perfectly well. So a row whose every typed word landed past the food's own name is dropped — but only where some other row answered on a strictly higher rung. That second condition is what makes it structural rather than a judgement call: the leading row can never be dropped, and a query no name answers leaves the bar at 0 so nothing is cut.

When your word reaches nothing at all

Only then — and the gate is absolute — a vocabulary map runs. It holds 444 phrases derived from Open Food Facts' ingredient taxonomy plus 8 hand-written British entries OFF does not carry: caster sugar, gammon, jacket potato, mange tout, natural yoghurt, plain flour, porridge oats, soymilk.

Why the gate is absolute

Because the expansion only runs when the literal search returned zero rows, an expansion can never reorder, displace or truncate a result you would have got anyway. The no-regression property is structural rather than a promise — which is also why the ranking has no key, tier or clause for the vocabulary at all.

09 · Keystroke, part two

Ten keys, read in order, never summed

Every retrieved row gets ten numbers. They are compared strictly in sequence — a tie on the first is broken by the second — so an earlier key is never traded against a later one. A row scores as the best of all its names, including the 79 rows carrying aliases a merge discarded.

The cascade, best-first · right column = leads that move if the key is removed

4accountedIs every word of the name answered? A boolean, because counting leftovers prefers “Chicken, ground” to a whole chicken.4

6plainSiblingDemote a row when a plainer version of it exists elsewhere in the corpus. Reaches 134 rows.29

Measured over a 3,857-query sweep built from every head phrase and head word in the corpus. Two of these — plainSibling and designated — read the row rather than its name, which is why a careless measuring harness loses them silently.

Why each of them exists

Not one was designed up front. Each arrived because a specific query led with a specific wrong row:

  • position — olive oil led with Oil, corn, peanut, and olive, a blend, for a query naming a single oil.
  • accounted — soybean oil led with Oil, soybean lecithin, an emulsifier, because two rows agreed on every key and the lower id won.
  • wholeness — australian beef led with external fat, and demoting that promoted seam fat at 562 kcal.
  • plainSibling — fifteen red and thirteen white varietal wines sitting above the wine they are varieties of.

12 · Honesty

How good is it, really

Before any of this was changed, a bar was written down and committed — 44 hand-judged queries, each with the exact record a diarist means, keyed by USDA id so a rename cannot fake a pass.

14 · Decided, not yet shipped

What was just concluded about the corpus

Typing beef today returns 954 rows whose first several are the same sentence permuted:

Beef, composite of trimmed retail cuts, separable lean and fat, trimmed to 1/8" fat, raw
Beef, composite of trimmed retail cuts, separable lean and fat, trimmed to 1/8" fat, choice, raw
Beef, composite of trimmed retail cuts, separable lean and fat, trimmed to 1/8" fat, select, raw

That list is not mis-sorted. It is one food written out thirty times, and no ordering of thirty copies produces fewer than thirty. Eight ranking keys were added over a year trying; none fixed it, because the problem was never the order.

A corpus row is a food as bought

An axis naming something true of the food when you bought it — variety, part, fat content — distinguishes a row. An axis naming something done to it afterwards, or a trade specification you never see — cooking, trim, grade, the butcher's separation — collapses onto the as-bought row.

You buy beef and cook it, so cooking collapses. You buy dried apricots as dried apricots, so drying distinguishes.

The test throughout is the meal-log test: two records are one food if you would write one word for them in a food diary. Not nutritional equivalence — raw and cooked beef differ by a median 31% and are still one food.

Both are true at once: beef answering with 188 butchery cuts is one row per ingredient, because a bottom round steak and a bottom round roast are two foods. The remaining distance is a paging problem, not a membership one.

The finding that shaped the rule

The obvious fix — delete every row naming a cooking method, then every row naming a trim or grade — takes the corpus to 1,873 and looks excellent. It also deletes 1,089 foods with no survivor and eighteen head phrases entirely: mutton, teff, spelt, turkey breast, and quinoa and apricots among them. They go because USDA publishes them only cooked or only trimmed, and a rule reading one row at a time cannot tell “this is a duplicate” from “this is the only record there is”.

The fix that reaches USDA's granularity is the fix that deletes quinoa.

And what it does not make redundant

The expectation was that collapsing the duplicates would make most of the machinery above pointless. It was measured, key by key and filter by filter. Nothing is redundant: all ten ranking keys still move leads, all fourteen drop rules still remove rows the collapse would not (3,735 of 3,736 discarded records would simply come back), and the four name rosters share no entry with the collapse roster.

The reason is structural. A collapse always leaves a survivor — it merges records of one food. Every rule above separates different foods: a fat from a meat, a brand from an ingredient, an imitation from the thing. That work is identical whether the corpus holds thirty copies of each or one.

15 · Questions

Questions this usually raises

How do I check whether a food I expected is missing?

Type it into the browser in section 7. If it is there, the row tells you which rule removed it and which words that rule fired on. If it is not there and it is not in the app either, it is a food USDA never published — the census covers every record in both archives, and its survivor count is checked against the shipped index on every regeneration, so it cannot quietly miss a record.

Why does egg lead with a duck egg?

Because Egg, duck, whole, fresh, raw reaches tier 50 exactly as a hen's egg does — the head phrase is your query in both — and the keys below tier cannot tell a common food from a rare one. There is no prevalence key, on purpose: the same key that would sink duck eggs would sink somebody's actual dinner.

Why is there no “popularity” ranking?

Nothing leaves the device, so there is no usage data to rank on. Ranking by assumed prevalence was considered and refused as a drop rule; it survives only as tie-breakers that fire on an exact tie in every earlier key. The standing principle is that prevalence may rank a food and may never drop one.

Is “the rule fired on this word” the same as “this word is in a denylist”?

No, and the difference matters. The census never reads the rules' word lists — they are private, and copying them would be a second copy of the judgement. It removes terms from the record until the real rule goes quiet, and reports the smallest set that keeps it quiet. So a term shown here is load-bearing for this verdict, which is a stronger and more useful claim than naming a table entry, and it survives a rewrite of the table.

Some rows show no terms at all. Why?

Because those rules do not read the name. superseded is a hand-written id list, no_energy reads the nutrient panel, the three variant rules ask what a row's head-phrase siblings look like, and the name-collision rules ask what other rows are called. There is no word to point at, and the census says so rather than inventing one.

Do I lose past logs when a row leaves the corpus?

No. A logged food carries its own nutrient panel into the append-only ledger, and nothing re-reads the search index for a past log. Dropping rows cannot damage history — which is why membership changes are safe to make and ordering changes are the fiddly ones.

Why are raw and cooked the same food, but whole and skimmed milk are not?

The meal-log test. You buy beef and cook it, so cooking happened after the purchase and collapses. You buy 2% milk as 2% milk, so fat content is true at purchase and distinguishes. Nutritional distance is explicitly not the test — a threshold on calorie movement was built and refuted, because cooking moves a chicken breast 13% and trim moves beef 12%, one point apart.

If a collapse throws away a measured difference, which number do I get?

One real USDA record's panel, whole — never a mean, because a mean is a number USDA never measured and no row can be pointed at to explain it. The survivor is chosen by fullest nutrient panel, then lowest id.

What happens when USDA has no good record?

The group ships its fullest-panel row under that row's whole, unstripped name. So Quinoa, cooked ships saying exactly that, rather than being renamed to Quinoa and quietly claiming to be the dry grain. A name may never claim less than its panel measures.

How do I regenerate this page?

pnpm usda:drop-census rebuilds the census from the archives in .usda-backup, then pnpm docs:food-search rebuilds this page from the census and the shipped index. pnpm docs:food-search --check fails if the committed page is out of date, and runs in pnpm check.
